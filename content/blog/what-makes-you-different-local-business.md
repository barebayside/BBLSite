Open your website and your three closest competitors' websites side by side. Family-owned. Reliable. Quality workmanship. Fully licensed. Free quotes. If you covered up the logos, could anyone tell you apart? We do this exercise in almost every competitor report we run, and most local businesses in the same trade are saying the same six things in a slightly different font. The fix isn't better adjectives — it's finding the one or two things a competitor could not honestly copy, and putting them where a customer actually looks. Here's how to find yours, and where to put it.

## Why "reliable and family-owned" isn't a difference

A differentiator has one test: **could your nearest competitor put the same sentence on their website tomorrow and not be lying?**

If yes, it isn't a differentiator. It's table stakes. Being licensed, insured, punctual and decent at the job is what a customer assumes before they ring anyone. You don't get credit for it — you only get punished for its absence.

That matters more than it used to, because people don't look at one business any more.

> **36% of consumers use two review sites when deciding on a local business, and 41% use three or more.** — BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey. At Bare Bayside Labs, this is why we compare a client against four or five named rivals rather than scoring them on their own: a customer is doing exactly that comparison, whether you are or not.

When someone is holding three tabs open, sameness is fatal. The three quotes look identical, so the decision falls to whichever is cheapest or answered first. If you've ever felt like you're constantly being reduced to a price, this is usually why.

## The four places a real difference actually comes from

Almost every genuine differentiator for a local business falls into one of four buckets. Work through them in order.

### 1. Something you do that they don't

Not "we do a good job" — an actual step in your process that has a name.

You send a photo of the completed work before you leave. You carry the three parts that cause 80% of callbacks so it's fixed the same visit. You do a free 12-month check on anything you install. You give a fixed price before you start, not an estimate that moves.

The test: it costs you something. If it were free and easy, everyone would already do it.

### 2. Something you're the only one qualified for

A specific accreditation, a manufacturer authorisation, a piece of equipment nobody else in the area owns, a licence class that's rare locally.

This is the strongest kind because it's checkable and it can't be claimed by anyone who doesn't have it.

### 3. A customer you understand better than anyone

Not a segment you'd like to have — one you already serve repeatedly.

Heritage homes with pre-1950 wiring. Cafés that can't shut during trade. Landlords with several properties who want one invoice. Families with a kid who's terrified of the dentist.

When you know a type of customer's specific problem, you can name it on the page. Everyone else writes for "customers".

### 4. Proof at a scale they can't match

Twelve years of before-and-afters in the same suburbs. Four hundred reviews that all mention the same thing. Photos of your own work rather than stock images.

This one compounds. It's also the one most businesses are sitting on and never show.

## The fastest differentiator most businesses ignore

If none of the four are obvious yet, start here, because it's available to everyone and almost nobody does it properly.

> **88% of consumers would use a business that replies to all of its reviews, against 47% for a business that never replies. 89% expect owners to reply to both positive and negative reviews.** — BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey. At Bare Bayside Labs, reply rate is one of the first things we measure, because the gap between a business that answers everything and one that answers nothing is close to double the consideration rate.

Answering every review, in your own words, within a day, is a differentiator hiding in plain sight. It takes minutes, it's visible on the page where people are already comparing you, and your competitors are mostly not doing it.

Speed of response works the same way. Expectations on how fast a business should reply keep climbing, and the business that answers first often wins before the others have read the email.

## Where to put it once you've found it

A differentiator nobody sees isn't doing anything. In order of impact:

**Your homepage, above the fold.** One sentence, in plain words, where the headline goes. Not in an "About Us" page three clicks in.

**Your Google Business Profile description and Services.** This is where comparison actually happens — most people never reach your website before forming a view.

**Every service page.** If your difference is a process step, describe it on the page for the service it applies to, not just once on the homepage.

**Your quotes.** The moment someone is holding three prices is the moment the difference has to be visible. Put it in the quote itself.

**Your review replies.** "Glad the same-day part swap worked out" says more about how you operate than a tagline does, and it says it to every future reader.

## How to say it without sounding like everyone else

Three rules that do most of the work.

**Be specific enough to be falsifiable.** "We reply to every enquiry within two hours during business hours" can be checked and held against you. "Fast, friendly service" can't. The checkable one is the one people believe.

**Name the thing you don't do.** "We don't do emergency callouts — we do planned work properly" repels the wrong customer and strongly attracts the right one. Trying to be for everybody is how you end up sounding like everybody.

**Cut every adjective you can't prove.** Delete "quality", "professional", "reliable", "trusted". If the sentence still means something afterwards, it was real. If it collapses, it was filler.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Picking a difference you can't sustain.** If it only works when you're not busy, it isn't a differentiator — it's a promise you'll break in summer.
- **Writing it for other tradies rather than customers.** Certifications and equipment names impress your peers. Say what it means for the person paying.
- **Choosing one and never checking whether rivals copied it.** Differences erode. Re-run the side-by-side once a year.
- **Confusing "different" with "cheaper".** Price is the one difference anyone can copy in an afternoon, and it's the only one that costs you money every single job.

## Key Takeaways

- **A differentiator is only real if a competitor couldn't honestly claim it too.** Licensed, reliable and family-owned are table stakes, not differences.
- **Customers compare 2-3+ sources before choosing**, so sameness pushes the decision to price or speed.
- **Look in four places:** a costly step in your process, a rare qualification, a customer type you know deeply, or proof at a scale others can't match.
- **Replying to every review is the fastest available differentiator** — 88% would use a business that replies to all reviews, against 47% for one that doesn't.
- **Put it above the fold, on your Google listing, on service pages and in your quotes** — not buried in an About page.
- **Be specific enough to be checked.** If it can't be falsified, it won't be believed.

## Common Questions

**What if I genuinely don't have anything different?**
You almost certainly have something you've stopped noticing because it's normal to you. Ask your last five customers why they picked you and what they'd tell a friend. The answer is usually a specific moment, not a quality.

**Can price be a differentiator?**
It can win jobs, but it's the weakest kind — anyone can undercut you tomorrow and you carry the cost forever. Being clear about price is far stronger than being cheapest; showing a "from" figure pre-qualifies enquiries and cuts time-wasters, even when you can't publish a fixed rate.

**How many differentiators should I have?**
One or two, said clearly. A list of eight reads as a list of none — nothing stands out and the reader remembers nothing.

**Do I need to change my logo or branding for this?**
No. This is about what you say and where you say it. A new logo changes nothing about whether a customer can tell you apart from the next quote.

**How often should I revisit this?**
Once a year, or any time a competitor visibly changes their offer. Differences erode as others catch up.
