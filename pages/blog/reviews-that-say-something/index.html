How to Get Reviews That Say Something (Not Just Five Stars)

[] 7 min read

You've got forty five-star reviews and they all say some version of "great service, highly recommend." Meanwhile the business down the road has twenty-five, and three of them describe exactly the job the customer reading them is about to book. Guess who gets the call. Star ratings get you shortlisted; the words are what closes it — and a review that names the job, the suburb and the outcome does work that a bare five stars can't. The good news is that review quality is almost entirely controlled by how you ask. Here's how to ask, and where the legal lines are.

1Why the words matter more than the number

The rating gets you onto the list. The reader then goes looking for someone whose situation matched theirs.

Reviews and star rating are a leading driver of who a buyer picks, and nearly all consumers read reviews across several sites before deciding. — BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey. At Bare Bayside Labs, review content is scored separately from review count for exactly this reason: a business can be ahead on volume and behind on whether the reviews say anything useful.

Think about how you choose a tradie yourself. You don't count the stars. You skim for someone who had your problem — the same appliance, the same age of house, the same emergency at the same hour — and you relax when you find them.

A specific review does three jobs a generic one can't:

  • It matches a reader's situation, so they picture the outcome.
  • It carries the words people actually search, which helps you turn up for those jobs.
  • It's obviously real. Twenty identical "great service" reviews read as suspicious. One that mentions a Tuesday night and a burst pipe in Wynnum reads as true.

2The bar keeps rising

Two things are moving against you at once.

Most consumers won't consider a local business below 4 stars, and the bar is climbing — a growing share now wants 4.5+. — BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey.

Buyers actively favour recent reviews, weighting the last few months most heavily, and review recency is a stronger local ranking factor than a big but stale total. — Whitespark, local ranking factors research. At Bare Bayside Labs, we look at the date of the newest review before we look at the count.

Together those mean a wall of glowing but ancient reviews is worth less than a steady trickle of recent, specific ones. Quality and recency compound; volume alone flattens out fast.

3How to ask so you get specifics

Almost all review quality is decided by the sentence you use when you ask. "Could you leave us a review?" reliably produces "Great service, thanks."

Ask one specific question instead

Replace the general ask with a prompt that has a natural answer.

Instead of "Could you leave a review?", try:

  • "If you leave a review, it'd help me a lot if you mentioned what the problem actually was — people search for that."
  • "Would you mind saying what we fixed and how long it took? That's the bit other customers want to know."
  • "If you could mention you're in Wynnum, that helps people nearby find us."

You're not scripting them. You're giving one hook so they don't fall back on the generic default.

Ask at the moment the detail is fresh

Ask when the customer can see the finished work and remembers what it was like before. A week later, the detail is gone and you get a summary. On the spot, you get a story.

Ask the customers with a story

Not every job produces a memorable review. The awkward access, the one you diagnosed when someone else couldn't, the after-hours callout, the job that came in under the quote — those customers have something to say. Ask them first.

Make it a 30-second job

Send the direct review link by text while you're still there. Every extra step — searching for the business, scrolling, working out where to tap — costs you reviews, and the ones you lose are disproportionately the detailed ones, because detail takes effort and effort needs momentum.

4The lines you must not cross

This is where well-meaning advice gets businesses in trouble, so be clear on it.

Never filter who you ask. Asking only the happy customers, or sending unhappy ones to a private feedback form instead of a public review, is called review gating and it breaks Google's policy directly.

Google's Maps user-generated-content policy prohibits selectively soliciting positive reviews and discouraging negative ones. — Google, Maps User Generated Content Policy. At Bare Bayside Labs, we won't build a review flow that gates, and reports flag it where we see it.

Never offer anything in exchange. Discounts, free work, entry into a draw, a coffee voucher — all of it breaks the same policy. Incentivised reviews are prohibited.

The consequences aren't limited to the reviews involved. Getting caught can escalate to profile suspension or lost visibility, and there are reports that Google began targeting gating tools in 2025. It's a poor trade for a handful of extra stars.

Never write them yourself, or have staff do it. Aside from being against policy, it's obvious. Fake reviews read like marketing copy because they are.

The honest version works better anyway: ask everyone, ask well, reply to whatever comes back.

5What to do with the reviews once you have them

Detailed reviews are an asset you've already paid for. Most businesses leave them sitting on Google.

Reply to every one, in your own words, naming the job. "Glad the hot water sorted out before the weekend, Sarah" is a reply. "Thanks for your feedback!" is not. Consumers expect owner replies, and templated ones undercut the benefit.

Put the specific ones on the matching service page. A review about a blocked drain belongs on the blocked-drain page, not the homepage carousel.

Use their words in your own copy. If four customers independently say "turned up when he said he would", that's your differentiator and your customers just wrote it for you.

Watch for the recurring theme. The thing several reviews mention without you prompting is what you're actually known for. It's usually not the thing on your homepage.

6Common mistakes to avoid

  • Asking by email a week later. Lowest response rate and the vaguest reviews. Ask in person, follow up by text.
  • Asking for "a five-star review". You're steering the rating, which is the thing policy prohibits. Ask for a review.
  • Chasing volume once you're comfortably ahead. Past a low baseline, more reviews stop lifting your ranking much — recency and content are where the remaining gain is.
  • Leaving negative reviews unanswered. A calm, specific reply to a bad review persuades more readers than the complaint does.

Key takeaways

  • Stars get you shortlisted; the words close the job. A review naming the job, suburb and outcome does work a bare five stars can't.
  • Review quality is set by how you ask. One specific question beats a general request every time.
  • Ask on the spot, while the detail is fresh, and make it a 30-second job with a direct link.
  • Never gate and never incentivise — both break Google's policy and can escalate to suspension.
  • Recency beats a big stale total. Buyers weight the last few months most heavily.
  • Reply to every review in your own words, and reuse the specific ones on the matching service page.

Common questions

Can I ask a customer to mention a specific service?

Yes. Asking someone to describe what you actually did is fine. What you can't do is tell them what rating to leave, or ask only the customers you expect to be positive.

What if I get a bad review?

Reply calmly, specifically and once. Readers weigh how you handled it more than the complaint itself. A single negative review among genuine positives also makes the whole set look more credible.

How many reviews do I need?

Roughly ten is a useful baseline where reviews start meaningfully helping local ranking. Past that, the gain from sheer volume tapers — put the effort into recency and content instead.

Is it OK to send customers a link to Google?

Yes. Sending everyone a direct link is fine and encouraged. It only becomes gating when you choose who gets it based on how happy you think they are.

Should I ask for reviews on more than one site?

Google first — it's where most local decisions happen. Once that's steady, a second platform helps, since a lot of consumers check two or more sources before deciding.
